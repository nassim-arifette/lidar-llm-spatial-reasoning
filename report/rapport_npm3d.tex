\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This report makes two contributions on nuScenes-QA \cite{nuscenesqa}, a
question-answering benchmark over driving scenes. The first is an audit of
its released answers. They do not distinguish left from right: with $a$ the
unsigned angle between the ego heading and the direction to an object,
``front left'' holds for $30^{\circ} < a \le 90^{\circ}$ on either side,
``back right'' for $90^{\circ} < a \le 150^{\circ}$ on either side, and
``front right'' and ``back left'' hold for no object. All $12{,}392$ counting
or existence questions of the official train and validation sets that name
one of the last two relations are answered $0$ or \emph{no}. ``Things''
include every annotation, the classes no question names among them, and the
ego vehicle; boxes with no LiDAR return are counted. On $1{,}460$ questions
from $146$ validation drives that played no part in finding these rules, a
deterministic executor agrees with the released answers on $85.1\%$ under the
published reading and on $97.5\%$ under these three conventions. The second
contribution is a comparison of two uses of one frozen language model on
ground-truth object lists: answering directly, or writing a JSON program that
a deterministic executor runs. Our earlier comparison stated the published
convention to both modes and scored both against labels that follow another
one. Executing the same programs under the convention of the labels moves six
models from $0.685$--$0.845$ to $0.765$--$0.980$ on $200$ questions from
three drives; the plateau near $0.845$ was the mismatch. On the $146$
validation drives, with both modes told the conventions of the labels,
Qwen2.5-7B reaches $0.961$ with programs against $0.595$ directly, a
difference of $+0.366$ with a $95\%$ drive-level bootstrap interval of
$[+0.337, +0.395]$, and Qwen2.5-1.5B $0.747$ against $0.582$. Asking the
direct mode to write out its working did not close the gap for these two
models. The rules are inferred from the answers, not read from the generation
code, and perception is an oracle throughout. The result concerns these
question families; it does not show that language models cannot reason about
space.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

nuScenes-QA \cite{nuscenesqa} pairs nuScenes keyframes \cite{nuscenes} with
templated questions about what is around the ego vehicle: how many, whether
any, in which of six directions, with which status. Systems such as LiDAR-LLM
\cite{lidarllm} are evaluated on it, and a number on its directional
questions is usually read as a measure of spatial reasoning.

This work started as a course project that reimplemented a reduced LiDAR-LLM
and could not train it on the hardware available (Section~\ref{sec:related}).
The project then fixed perception and compared two uses of one frozen
language model: \emph{direct}, where the model reads an object list and
answers, and \emph{program}, where it writes a small JSON program and a
deterministic executor computes the answer from the same list. A first
version of this report presented that comparison under the relations as the
benchmark paper defines them, six signed $60^{\circ}$ sectors. In that
version the program mode stopped near $0.845$ for every model but the smallest, and we attributed the executor's remaining
disagreements with the reference answers to objects near sector boundaries.

That attribution was wrong. The disagreements came from a convention of the
labels that the published definition does not state. This revision is
organised around what followed.

\paragraph{Contribution 1: an audit of the labels
(Section~\ref{sec:audit}).} Three conventions of the released answers differ
from what the paper's text and the question wording suggest: left and right
are not distinguished; ``things'' are every annotation plus the ego vehicle;
objects with no LiDAR return are counted. Each is stated as a rule and tested
on data that played no part in finding it, including the official train set.

\paragraph{Contribution 2: direct against program once the conventions are
right (Section~\ref{sec:aslabelled}).} The archived comparison scored both
modes against labels that follow a convention neither was told. We
re-execute the archived programs under the convention of the labels, rerun
the direct mode with a prompt that states it, add an ablation where the
direct mode writes out its working, treat constrained decoding as a separate
condition, and repeat the comparison on $146$ validation drives with
intervals that resample drives. The archived numbers stay in
Section~\ref{sec:published}, labelled as results under the published reading.

\paragraph{Limits, stated once here and again where they apply.} Perception
is an oracle: object lists are ground-truth annotations. The first comparison
has three evaluation drives. Prompts and the layout were developed on
nuScenes v1.0-mini. The left/right rule was found by looking at evaluation
failures and then confirmed on held-out data; the ``things'' rule was found
on the validation drives and confirmed on the train set. The rules describe
the answers; the generation code was not read. The $32$B model is quantised
to $4$ bits.

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:method}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Related work and origin of the project}
\label{sec:related}

CLEVR \cite{clevr} represents a question as a functional program over a small
catalogue of operations (filter, unique, relate, count, exist, query,
compare) executed on a scene graph, and neural-symbolic VQA \cite{nsvqa}
showed that recovering and executing such a program is far more accurate on
CLEVR than predicting the answer end to end. The comparison in this report
reproduces that finding on real driving annotations with frozen models and no
training; the idea is not ours. Closer in domain, ZSVG3D \cite{zsvg3d}
generates programs for zero-shot 3D grounding and KLDrive \cite{kldrive}
combines scene facts with constrained LLM reasoning. Their perception and protocols differ from ours, so they give
no comparable numbers. Real-3DQA \cite{real3dqa} shows that on
SQA3D a model fine-tuned with no 3D input matches or beats existing 3D-LLMs,
and that filtering blind-answerable questions drops LEO's exact match from
$49.4\%$ to $14.3\%$; the scene-dependence control of Section~\ref{sec:scene}
follows that argument.

The first version of the project implemented LiDAR-LLM \cite{lidarllm} in
reduced form on $323$ training samples, without a GPU. It showed that
gradients flow and nothing more, and its evaluation had no question-only
control and split questions rather than scenes. The present protocol was
designed against those two weaknesses.

\subsection{Scenes, programs and the executor}

A scene is a list of objects, each with a class (the ten nuScenes detection
classes), a centre, a box size, a yaw and, where the annotation carries one,
a \emph{status}: moving, parked, stopped, standing, sitting or lying down,
with rider, without rider. Coordinates are in the ego frame, $x$ forward,
$y$ left. An unknown status is treated as neither the attribute nor its
negation: a traffic cone is neither ``standing'' nor ``not standing''.

A program is an operation (\textsc{count}, \textsc{exists},
\textsc{compare}) over a selector: a category or category group, an optional
status, an optional region (one of the six relations, near, far, or a
radius), and an optional anchor that makes the region relative to another
object. Two parsers emit this language. A deterministic rule grammar produces
reference programs and declines what it cannot represent; the frozen language
model is the system under test.

Execution is a filter followed by an arithmetic operation. It has no learned
parameters. A definite reference whose resolution is ambiguous (``the
truck'' when the scene has three) is refused, not resolved by taking the
first match. Refusals, invalid programs and unreadable answers count as wrong
everywhere in this report. Objects closer than $1$\,m to the ego origin are
excluded from directional queries. Relations relative to an object (``to the
front of the bus'') keep the ego vehicle's heading and move the origin to
that object.

The executor takes the spatial convention as a named \emph{layout}. Two
matter here: \code{nuscenes\_qa}, the six signed $60^{\circ}$ sectors of
Equation~2 of the benchmark paper, which we call the \emph{published}
reading, and \code{nuscenes\_qa\_as\_labelled}, the rule of
Section~\ref{sec:leftright}, which we call \emph{as labelled}. A program
names a relation and not a layout, so the same generated program can be
executed under either.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{First comparison.} One set of questions was fixed before either
mode ran, in the manifest \code{first\_comparison.json}: $100$ development
and $200$ evaluation questions from nuScenes-QA on v1.0-mini, split by drive (seven
development drives, three evaluation drives, no overlap). Questions are
selected by what they ask, from the surface structure of the text, and not by
what a parser manages: $1{,}626$ of the $5{,}916$ mini questions are in
scope. The scope is what the rule grammar can express, because the grammar
supplies the reference programs against which the executor is checked before
any model runs. There are four families: category only (``are there any
cars''), category with a status (``how many parked cars''), category in a
region relative to the ego vehicle, and category anchored on another object.

\paragraph{Validation drives.} A second set, \code{val\_drives.json}, was built in November 2026 by the same
rules from the official nuScenes-QA validation questions on the v1.0-trainval
annotations: $1{,}460$ questions, ten from each of $146$ drives, none of
which is in v1.0-mini. It has no development part; development remains the
development split of the first comparison.

\paragraph{Matched conditions.} Both modes use the same checkpoint through
one runtime, the same precision and greedy decoding. The direct mode receives
the object rows (id, category, $x$, $y$, status) in an order that does not
depend on the question, with no pre-filtering and no precomputed counts. Both
prompts state the ego frame and the treatment of unknown status; the direct
prompt also states the spatial convention in prose, exactly as the executor
computes it. Prompt \code{direct-1} states the published signed sectors and
\code{direct-2} the as-labelled rule. Prompts longer than the context budget
are refused, not trimmed, and every generation records whether it stopped on
its own; in the archived runs, at a $256$-token budget, no generation was
truncated.

\paragraph{Oracle perception.} Object lists are ground-truth annotations, not
detections. This fixes perception so that the comparison is about what each
mode does with a scene. Nothing here speaks to detection quality.

\section{An audit of the nuScenes-QA answers}
\label{sec:audit}
% =====================================================================

Each convention below was found while explaining disagreements between the
executor and the reference answers, then tested on data that played no part
in finding it. They are descriptions of the released labels.

\subsection{The spatial relations do not distinguish left from right}
\label{sec:leftright}

Let $a \in [0^{\circ}, 180^{\circ}]$ be the unsigned angle between the ego
heading and the direction to the object. In the released answers, an object
is to the \emph{front} when $a \le 30^{\circ}$; to the \emph{front left} when
$30^{\circ} < a \le 90^{\circ}$, on either side of the vehicle; to the
\emph{back right} when $90^{\circ} < a \le 150^{\circ}$, on either side; to
the \emph{back} when $a > 150^{\circ}$. No object is ever to the \emph{front
right} or to the \emph{back left}. This is what the arccosine of a normalised
dot product, the form of Equation~2 of the benchmark paper, gives when the
sign of the angle is never recovered.

\paragraph{Evidence without geometry.} Take every counting or existence
question of the official release that names exactly one relation, and count
how often the answer is $0$ or \emph{no} (Table~\ref{tab:nogeom}). For four
relations the rate is between $20\%$ and $24\%$. For ``front right'' and
``back left'' it is $100\%$: $12{,}392$ questions across $34{,}117$
keyframes, and not one has an object there. The same holds on v1.0-mini
($37/37$ and $41/41$). This check uses the question files alone.

\paragraph{What the first version of this report said instead.} On the
development split the executor under signed sectors agreed on $50/50$
non-directional questions, $17/18$ axial ones and $24/32$ diagonal ones, with
``front left'' alone at $5/10$. We described this as a residual at the
boundaries of the diagonal sectors and categorised eight of the nine
development disagreements that way. That categorisation is superseded: those
eight are the left/right convention, and the ninth is the non-unique
reference above. An archived diagnostic on $68$ ego-anchored evaluation
questions had compared signed candidates only (uniform $60^{\circ}$ sectors
$76.5\%$, mirrored $72.1\%$, camera frusta $67.6\%$, octants $61.8\%$); it
could not find a rule outside the family it searched.

\subsection{``Things'' are every annotation, and the ego vehicle is one}
\label{sec:things}

For ``How many things are there?'' the answer equals the number of
annotations of the keyframe, of every category, plus one. This holds for all
$2{,}080$ such questions of the official train set and all $648$ of the
validation set. The alternatives fail: every annotation without the extra one
matches no question in either set, the ten named classes plus one match
$1{,}610$ and $495$, and annotations with a LiDAR return plus one match $973$
and $304$. A ``thing'' therefore includes classes that no question names
(pushable objects, debris, bicycle racks, animals), includes boxes with no
LiDAR return, and includes the ego vehicle. The ego vehicle also counts in
relations between objects (``how many things are to the back of the bus''),
though never to the front or back \emph{of me}. This was found on the
validation drives of Section~\ref{sec:protocol} and confirmed on the train
set, which had not been looked at before.

The object-list option \code{things="all"} implements it: unnamed classes are
kept as \code{other}, the ego vehicle is appended at the origin, and both
match only ``thing'' or ``object''. Every archived run used the ten named
classes; on v1.0-mini the difference is one question in $300$.
Table~\ref{tab:valexec} gives the executor's agreement with rule-grammar
reference programs on the $1{,}460$ validation-drive questions under the four
combinations.

\subsection{Objects with no LiDAR return are counted}
\label{sec:lidar}

v1.0-mini, $4{,}278$ of $18{,}538$ annotations ($23\%$) have none, and the
answers count them. Holding the programs fixed and changing only the object
list gives Table~\ref{tab:lidar}. The first block is the table of the first
version of this report. It was produced by an earlier version of the rule

rows are the current code, under each layout. The conclusion is the same in
every row: keeping only objects the sensor returned roughly halves accuracy
on counting questions. This is a mismatch between the labels and a
LiDAR-only input, not a measured detector error. Every experiment below uses

\subsection{What this means for a user of the benchmark, and limits}
\label{sec:meaning}

A system can score well on the directional questions with no notion of left
and right, and a system that has one is penalised: a correct count of the
cars to the front right is marked wrong whenever there is one. A LiDAR-only
system is asked to count objects its sensor did not return and objects of
classes a ten-class detector does not have. Results reported on nuScenes-QA
remain comparable with each other, since every system faces the same labels.
What changes is what a number means: it measures agreement with these
conventions, not left/right spatial reasoning. A method that encodes the
published geometry, as our executor did, is capped well below $100\%$ for a
reason unrelated to its quality.

The limits are these. The rules are inferred from answers; reading the
generation code could confirm or refine them. The validation-set check with
geometry uses unverified rule-grammar programs. Some questions still disagree
under all three conventions: $4$ of $200$ on the mini evaluation split and
$36$ of $1{,}460$ on the validation drives. On the validation drives these
are $19$ directional questions, mostly counts off by one or two, $8$
questions whose reference object is not unique or not found, $7$ status
questions and $2$ plain counts. They were not investigated further.

executor and the data. The plateau is real under this reading: $28$ of the
$200$ questions are wrong in program mode for all six models, so no model
could pass $0.860$. It is not a property of the task or of program writing.
It is the convention mismatch (Section~\ref{sec:reexec}).

\emph{One hosted model reaches parity.} \code{qwen3.8-27b} answered directly
at $0.840$ against $0.845$ with programs, while reporting $523.9$ output
tokens per direct question against $2.0$ for the other hosted models; it
reasons before answering, and it exceeded the requested $256$ tokens freely.
We read this as a boundary of the program advantage. Section~\ref{sec:hosted}
re-reads it.

\subsection{The scene-dependence control}
\label{sec:scene}

Thirteen evaluation questions of the first comparison appear verbatim on two
scenes with different reference answers. A system that does not read the
objects gives the same answer to both. Under the published reading, the
direct mode of Qwen2.5-1.5B gave one answer to a whole group in $4$ of $13$
groups and was correct across a group once; its program mode never gave one
answer to a whole group and was correct across all $13$ (on development,
$5$ groups: direct correct across none, program across $4$). The two hosted
single-step models were correct across $3$ of $13$ groups directly and $13$
with programs, and \code{qwen3.8-27b} across $13$ in both modes. The
re-execution as labelled leaves the $1.5$B program mode at $13$ of $13$. No
aggregate accuracy shows this, and it is the kind of control Real-3DQA
\cite{real3dqa} argues for.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{What the audit establishes.} The released nuScenes-QA answers
follow three conventions that the question wording does not suggest. The
left/right finding needs no geometry and no executor to verify: $12{,}392$
of $12{,}392$ questions is a property of the question files. Anyone reporting
numbers on the benchmark is measuring agreement with these conventions. A
system built on the published definition, or on a correct notion of left and
right, loses points for it, and a comparison between such a system and one
trained on the answers is biased against the first. We do not know why the
labels have this form and make no claim about the generation code.

\paragraph{What the comparison establishes.} For these question families, at
fixed oracle perception and a fixed frozen model, executing a program the
model writes is far more reliable than having the model answer from the
object list. On $146$ held-out drives the difference is $+0.366$ $[+0.337,
+0.395]$ at $7$B and $+0.166$ $[+0.125, +0.205]$ at $1.5$B. Telling the
direct mode the right convention moved it by a few points, and asking it to
write out its working did not close the gap for the $1.5$B and $7$B models.
Once the convention is right, the program mode of every model but the
smallest is between $0.950$ and $0.980$ on the first comparison, against a
ceiling of $0.980$.

\paragraph{What our first version got wrong, and why it matters beyond this
report.} We implemented the published geometry, measured $91$ of $100$
agreement, read the nine disagreements, and filed eight of them as sector
boundary effects. The explanation was plausible and wrong, and it produced a
second wrong statement: that the program mode saturates near $0.845$. The
as-labelled rule was then found by looking at evaluation failures, which is
why every check of it reported here uses other data.

\paragraph{What is not established.} Nothing here shows that language models
cannot reason about space. The direct mode is asked to count rows in a table
of several dozen objects, a task small models are known to be poor at, and
part of its disadvantage may be context handling and not spatial reasoning.
The reasoned-direct ablation covers two models, one prompt and one budget.
The one model that reasons at length by default was not rerun under the
corrected prompt, and the thinking-switch experiment is designed but not yet
complete. Perception is an oracle, so the result says nothing about a system
that must detect objects first; given Section~\ref{sec:lidar}, such a system
would also be asked to count objects it cannot see. The first comparison has
three evaluation drives, and the prompts and layout were developed on
v1.0-mini. The $32$B model is quantised to $4$ bits. On the wide question
scope, at $1.5$B, the program mode is not ahead.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

The released nuScenes-QA answers do not distinguish left from right, count
the ego vehicle and unnamed classes as things, and count objects without a
LiDAR return. We give the rules, evidence on the official train and
validation sets, and their limits: they are inferred from answers.

These conventions changed the reading of our own experiment. Under the
published definition the program mode appeared to saturate near $0.845$ and
one reasoning model appeared to match it by answering directly. Under the
convention the labels follow, the same programs reach $0.950$ to $0.980$ for
five of six models, the questions no model gets right fall from $28$ to $4$
of $200$, and on $146$ held-out drives a $7$B model answers $0.961$ of the
questions through programs and $0.595$ directly. The practical consequence
for these question families is to let the language model write the query and
let code compute the answer, and to check what a benchmark's labels encode
before interpreting a ceiling.

$1{,}580$ per-question records, are kept unchanged; the offline

counterparts. The runs of November 2026 are in
\code{results/runs\_2026\_11}, each with its commit, command, GPU and package
versions. Nothing is trained; the $1.5$B comparison runs on a T4. Tables
\ref{tab:direct}, \ref{tab:reasoned}, \ref{tab:constrained}, \ref{tab:val}
